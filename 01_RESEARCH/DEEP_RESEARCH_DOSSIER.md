# HOW AI IS CHANGING WORK
## Jobs, Skills, Pay & Career Paths Across 271 Occupations
### Deep research dossier — 16 September 2026

## Research promise
This project is for people who keep hearing that AI will “change jobs” or “take jobs” but do not necessarily know what that means. It compares familiar professions — design, education, healthcare, finance, media, legal/professional services, customer support, research, engineering and more — and separates questions public discussion often mixes together:

1. **Exposure:** could current AI affect part of this job?
2. **Adoption:** are people actually using AI for the work?
3. **Automation vs augmentation:** does AI perform the task, or help a person perform it?
4. **Productivity:** does work become faster, broader or higher quality?
5. **Demand:** is employment in the profession projected to grow or shrink?
6. **Skills:** what capabilities become more or less valuable?
7. **Career path:** what changes for beginners versus experienced workers?
8. **Pay/value:** do productivity gains translate into worker earnings?

These are related, but they are not interchangeable.

# 1. Target audience
**Primary:** people roughly 18–35 — students, people choosing careers, early-career workers, career switchers and young professionals — who have heard AI is affecting work but do not follow AI or labour-market research closely.

**Secondary:** educators, managers, recruiters, parents and general visitors.

**Communication rule:** plain-language question → visual interaction → evidence → optional technical peel.

# 2. Core research question
## **When AI enters a profession, what changes first — the tasks, the skills, the demand for the job, the career path, or the value of the worker?**

### Core thesis
**AI exposure is not a job-decline score.**

AI reaches different tasks unevenly. Actual AI use is different from theoretical exposure. A highly exposed profession can still grow; a less-exposed profession can decline for other reasons. Outcomes depend on task mix, human abilities, demand, worker experience, organisational choices and wider economic forces.

# 3. Central quantitative discovery: exposure barely predicts projected job growth
The uploaded occupation file contains **271 occupations**.

**AI exposure vs projected U.S. employment growth, 2024–2034: r = -0.094**

Among the **90 occupations labelled High exposure**:
- **72 (80.0%)** have positive projected growth.
- **47 (52.2%)** grow faster than the BLS all-occupation baseline of 3.1%.
- **13 (14.4%)** decline.

The correct insight is:
> **Exposure alone is a poor shortcut for whether a profession is growing or shrinking.**

BLS projects total U.S. employment to grow **3.1%** from 2024–2034, adding roughly **5.2 million jobs**. [S03]

### High exposure + growth examples
- Operations research analysts — exposure 0.629; growth +21%
- Financial examiners — 0.576; +19%
- Logisticians — 0.597; +17%
- Computer & information systems managers — 0.561; +15%
- Personal financial advisors — 0.671; +10%
- Public relations specialists — 0.788; +5%

### High exposure + decline examples
- Survey researchers — 0.844; −5%
- Customer service representatives — 0.705; −5%
- Advertising sales agents — 0.692; −6%
- News analysts/reporters/journalists — 0.633; −4%

This becomes the central **FOUR FUTURES OF WORK** infographic. Never label quadrants “safe” or “doomed.”

# 4. Exposure relates more strongly to the kind of thinking inside work
Correlations in the 271-occupation file:
- Verbal ability: **r = 0.711**
- Reasoning: **r = 0.491**
- Quantitative: **r = 0.432**
- Memory: **r = 0.386**
- Spatial: **r = -0.394**
- Median wage: **r = 0.286**
- Cognitive demand: **r = 0.470**

Current LLM-style exposure measures reach language, information and structured cognitive work more directly than work rooted in physical space. This does not make spatial/physical jobs “AI-proof.”

OECD likewise distinguishes exposure from automation risk: some high-skill jobs are highly exposed but remain hard to automate because of non-routine cognitive and social requirements. [S26]

# 5. There is no single perfect “AI risk score”
Human-rated and GPT-4-rated exposure in the uploaded file correlate at **r = 0.835** — strong agreement overall, imperfect agreement occupation by occupation.

The platform should show:
- human LLM exposure
- GPT-4 LLM exposure
- ability-based AIOE

as **three lenses**, not three probabilities of layoff.

# 6. Global exposure: transformation is more plausible than wholesale replacement
ILO's 2025 refined GenAI index analyses nearly 30,000 tasks. It estimates about **1 in 4 workers globally** are in occupations with some GenAI exposure, while **3.3%** of global employment is in its highest exposure category. ILO emphasizes that most exposed occupations still require human input, so transformation is more likely than automatic redundancy. [S01]

**EXPOSED ≠ AUTOMATED ≠ REPLACED**

# 7. Possible exposure is not actual use
- OpenAI/UPenn's theoretical exposure framework estimated ~80% of U.S. workers could have at least 10% of tasks affected and ~19% at least half — a capability estimate, not a job-loss forecast. [S05]
- Anthropic's initial observed-usage study found Claude use across at least a quarter of tasks in ~36% of occupations in its sample; task interactions leaned **57% augmentation / 43% automation**. Platform data are not representative of all workers. [S07]
- Microsoft Research analysed **200,000 anonymized Copilot conversations**; common user activities included information gathering and writing, while AI commonly provided information/assistance, writing, teaching and advising. [S09]

# 8. AI in real professions

## Education
Gallup/Walton: **60%** of U.S. public K–12 teachers used AI for work in 2024–25; **32%** used it at least weekly. Weekly users estimated saving **5.9 hours/week**. [S16]  
A 2026 follow-up found only **18%** received formal AI-use guidance. [S17]

## Healthcare
AMA 2026: **81%** of surveyed physicians reported professional AI use. Common uses: research summaries 39%, discharge/care/progress text 30%, documentation 28%, chart summaries 28%, patient-message drafts 19%, translation 18%, assistive diagnosis 17%. [S18]

## Creative/design work
Adobe 2026 combined job postings, **1,433 working creatives/creators**, **3,300 people training to enter creative work**, and qualitative research. Treat as vendor-sponsored evidence. [S19]

It reports:
- creative-professional postings +8% in its six-month comparison;
- explicit AI requirements 10% → 15%;
- 84.8% positive sentiment around AI for brainstorming/ideation;
- photo professionals: 57.6% substantial GenAI use for background removal, 50% compositing, 45.1% colour/tone retouching;
- 24.1% GenAI use in reviews with collaborators.

Its qualitative interpretation: value can move toward **selection, direction, client communication and accountability**.

## Professional services
Thomson Reuters 2026: **40%** said their organizations used GenAI, up from 22%; **80%+ of current users** used it weekly; only **18%** said their organizations tracked AI ROI. [S20]

## Customer support
NBER field study, **5,179 agents**: AI assistance increased productivity **14% on average** and **34% among novice/lower-skilled workers**. [S10]

# 9. Productivity does not automatically become higher earnings
NBER Denmark: rapid adoption, reported productivity benefits and task reorganisation, but **no measurable average earnings or recorded-hours effect within two years**, ruling out effects larger than **2%**. [S11]

Pew adds a subjective workplace layer: among U.S. workers who had used AI chatbots for work, **40%** found them extremely/very helpful for speed vs **29%** for quality. [S15]

Website question:
## **FASTER. THEN WHAT?**

# 10. Early career is a separate problem
Stanford 2026 ADP data: no widespread economy-wide displacement, but U.S. workers aged **22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations** were **19% below** where employment would have been if it had kept pace with less-exposed peers; experienced workers showed no comparable gap. [S12]

This is observational and does not prove AI caused the broader youth-employment slowdown. OECD notes that broader deterioration began before current LLMs. [S26]

PwC 2026: highly AI-exposed U.S. junior roles were **7× more likely** to request traditionally senior skills like judgement and leadership. [S14]

Question, not conclusion:
> **If beginner tasks accelerate, how do beginners build judgement?**

# 11. Skills: technical fluency is only one layer
WEF 2025 employer survey:
- **39%** of workers' core skills expected to change by 2030;
- **59 in 100 workers** estimated to need training;
- **63% of employers** cite skills gaps as a primary barrier. [S13]

OECD emphasizes a mix of foundational, ICT/AI and complementary skills: problem solving, creative thinking, communication, collaboration and emotional intelligence. [S26]

Better model:
**AI fluency × domain expertise × judgement × verification × communication × responsibility**

# 12. India
IMF estimates **26% of Indian workers** in high-AI-exposure occupations:
- **14%** relatively complementary
- **12%** higher displacement risk [S21]

World Bank WDR 2026:
- estimated GenAI automation risk: **4.5%** of existing jobs in low/middle-income economies vs **14.2%** in high-income economies;
- potential meaningful productivity gains: **16.2%** of jobs in developing economies vs **18.7%** in high-income economies. [S27]

NASSCOM 2026 technology-industry research reports **20–40% of work** across participating Indian tech organisations being done through AI across functions. Sector-specific; not an all-India statistic. [S23]

# 13. What workers feel
Pew U.S. worker survey:
- 52% worried
- 36% hopeful
- 33% overwhelmed
- 29% excited about future workplace AI use. [S15]

The target audience therefore needs clarity rather than hype.

# 14. Qualitative mechanisms
ILO Poland mixed-methods research:
- employer formal introduction 9.4% vs worker use in previous week 16.7%;
- experienced users treated verification as a normal part of work;
- occasional users voiced more concern about creativity and skill erosion. [S28]

Use these as paraphrased **qualitative themes**, not invented quotes.
